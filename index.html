01 / Context

Behind the
Identification

A plant photograph. A suggested name.
Someone still has to decide whether to agree.

Meet the people

This imaginary participatory study revolves around people contributing to Auckland plant records on iNaturalist NZ: observers, identifiers and those supporting their participation (iNaturalist NZ, n.d.). Remote identifiers are within this activity-based community, so Auckland residence is not a membership criterion.

iNaturalist’s computer vision suggests names from photographs, and other users can agree. The platform asks contributors to verify an identification, rather than agree from gratitude or rely solely on AI, because careless agreement can wrongly make a record Research Grade (iNaturalist, n.d.). Auckland Council uses residents’ weed records to set priorities on Aotea (Auckland Council, 2024), so a wrong name matters beyond the app. Understanding this shared judgement requires understanding the people involved.

Research question

How do observers and identifiers contributing to Auckland plant records on iNaturalist NZ decide when to trust AI suggestions and other users’ identifications?

02 / Participants

Four positions. Shared ground.

These are proposed research segments, not fixed identities. Under Salmon et al.’s (2021) citizen–scientist–enabler framework, one person may observe, identify and facilitate. Selection would involve contributors’ descriptions of their experiences, rather than a leaderboard.

Roles overlap

Newer users contributing Auckland plant records would illuminate first encounters with suggestions, correction and community conventions. Platform inexperience would not imply botanical inexperience.

Read profile

Repeat local recorders would bring knowledge of places, changing vegetation and why they document particular plants—complementing occasional encounters, but not representing all residents.

Read profile

People identifying others’ Auckland plant records would explain reviewing evidence and communicating uncertainty. Remote and local contributors are both crucial, as both can assess these records.

Read profile

NZBRN support staff and local iNaturalist plant educators would explain guidance and participation barriers. Council data users remain contextual stakeholders, but not an automatic fifth group.

03A / Newer observers

Learning to take part

At Cornwall Park, the advertised Wonder walls activity is for beginners and experienced iNaturalist contributors alike, with experts available to help. The park’s ecology walk introduces field guides, apps and identification keys (Cornwall Park, n.d.). These invitations position participation as something learned with others, not a skill all visitors possess.

Lowe et al. (2025) surveyed 429 users, 208 through US-focused quota sampling: 64.3% were under 45 and 76% held higher-education degrees. Species knowledge and ecosystem discovery were prominent motivators across participation levels. These figures describe respondents, not Auckland’s contributors, but suggest a relatively young, educated group who join to learn.

A public forum newcomer, whose location is unspecified, asked how to update a record after a tree was removed. Cataloguing invasive species was “the main reason I signed up for iNaturalist”; they also wanted to learn about garden weeds (mtxo, 2021, paras. 2–3). This account suggests curiosity can exist alongside concern for useful records. In this study, newcomers would therefore be collaborators who have questions and purposes of their own, not just people needing correction.

03B / Local observers

Returning to the same place

Ford’s (2020) Meadowbank report documents repeated visits between April 2018 and March 2020. It combines habitats, iNaturalist links and identification assistance. This is a concrete Auckland practice: observing involves deciding what context to preserve, not just obtaining a species name. The published entry below offers three readings.

Published record

Vernonia mespilifolia

01
“One large plant seen by stream, potentially planted (Identified by Rhys Gardner).” (Ford, 2020, p. 120)

The entry places a large plant by a stream and keeps the doubt about planting: context beyond a name.

Ford credits Rhys Gardner: the name rests on a named person’s help, with no AI suggestion recorded.

The report links to an iNaturalist observation: field notes joined to a shared record.

A contributor identifying their locality as North Auckland describes recording plant losses and ecological threats, with more observations than they can index or follow up. Their hope is for “more people in this locality” to help observe and follow change (kaipatiki_naturewatc, 2021, paras. 1, 5). This situated voice combines environmental concern, shared responsibility and practical limits.

Sustained observers would contribute that context, though repeated observation does not establish expertise across all plants.

03C / Identifiers

Looking twice at a name

Identifiers contribute attention as well as taxonomic knowledge. Campbell et al.’s (2023) global analysis found that identification is assisted by a small subset of highly active, often specialised contributors. This supports distinguishing experience by the plants someone identifies, rather than taking contribution totals as universal expertise.

iNaturalist’s December 2025 survey invited people with at least 2,000 active identifications for others; nearly 2,500 answered all or part of it. Learning, data accuracy and giving back were prominent motives. Respondents could describe themselves in several ways, so “Researcher or scientist”, “Student” and “Nature lover” were overlapping identities, not competing categories (tiwane, 2026).

“it’s really satisfying to be able to contribute to the community while learning”

(tiwane, 2026, Question 3)

Respondents also described inadequate photographic evidence and the work of correcting automated suggestions (tiwane, 2026). Satisfaction and frustration co-exist: this is neither an infallible expert service nor unlimited volunteer time. In the proposed study, identifiers’ accounts would be considered alongside observers’ experiences, rather than used as the official verdict on them.

03D / Enablers

Opening a way in

The New Zealand Bio-Recording Network Trust operates iNaturalist NZ with ecology and biodiversity-IT expertise. Its help service distinguishes onboarding and technical troubleshooting from species-identification requests (iNaturalist NZ, n.d.). Therefore, support workers bring knowledge of participation challenges, without necessarily being the people who identify a plant.

Kaipātiki Project’s Hands On Ecology programme, facilitated by Te Taiao Practitioner Charmaine Bailie, includes plant identification and iNaturalist recording for people “interested in learning about restoration ecology through a te ao Māori lens” (Kaipātiki Project, n.d., para. 1). Its focus on local reserves and cultural relationships suggests why educators are more than app demonstrators.

Auckland Council’s Aotea initiative provides another connection: its Natural Environment Delivery Islands team uses weed observations to inform management priorities and accepts emailed photographs as an alternative (Auckland Council, 2024).

Relevant educators would help explore how identification advice is introduced; I would keep organisational aims distinct from individuals’ values. Their voices would complement—not speak for—learners and identifiers.

04 / Positionality

My place in the picture

4A · My standpoint

As a university student working from public sources, I can compare evidence without claiming membership or first-hand knowledge of this community. My question foregrounds AI. However, the accounts above also concern learning, place and limited capacity (kaipatiki_naturewatc, 2021; Lowe et al., 2025). I need to ask whether my framing serves contributors or mainly my interest in technology.

Identifiers who have reviewed thousands of records hold expertise I cannot claim, so I would be a learner, not a judge. Assessment creates another stake: a clear argument and a good grade. I could select convenient quotations or tidy overlapping roles into artificial categories. I would keep an interpretation log recording evidence, assumptions and disagreements, including cases that resist my explanation. Academic affiliation should not make my interpretation the final authority.

A technical lens asks whether an identification is trustworthy, but can miss whether a newcomer feels entitled to question a prolific identifier: confidence in knowledge differs from feeling safe with others (University of Auckland, 2026b).

A participatory lens asks whose concerns shape the research. Contributors would help revise the question, following the lectures’ continuing-engagement approach (University of Auckland, 2026a); this does not mean persuading anyone to trust AI.

4B · From insight to method

Because newcomers may already know plants well, I would separate botanical and platform experience and explain that participation is not a competence test. Because local recorders may record more than they can follow up, I would invite brief written or spoken responses; because identification leans on a few prolific volunteers, I would include less-visible contributors. Before recruitment, I would seek ethics approval and agree with enablers such as Kaipātiki Project on consent, attribution, sensitive locations and cultural knowledge.

Brief, semi-structured discussions of participant-selected plant records would ground questions in familiar experience.

Participants could challenge my interpretations and choose a useful return, such as a reviewed summary. Removing a username does not guarantee anonymity. Understanding the people behind a record means sharing authority over how their contributions are represented.

Generative AI disclosure

YES — I used generative AI tools while undertaking this assignment.

1. Which AI tools did you use?
I used Claude (Anthropic) to support my research and source discovery, to refine my written English, and to build this interactive photo-essay website.
2. What did you use these tools for?
Claude helped me find relevant academic literature, government publications, demographic statistics and publicly available community information about iNaturalist NZ. I also used it to check whether sources were relevant to my question, to make my English clearer, and to develop the HTML, CSS and JavaScript behind the site. The photographs are credited source photographs, not AI-generated images.
3. What prompts did you use?
I asked Claude to identify academic and official sources on iNaturalist NZ, to compare the evidence about different participant groups, to review my draft against the assignment rubric, and to suggest clearer English phrasing. I also asked it to implement the interactive features, such as the participant role map and the observation record card.
4. How did you verify the information and sources provided by AI?
I went back to the original academic articles, official iNaturalist documentation, government publications and community sources before using anything from them. I kept findings about Auckland separate from studies of the wider iNaturalist population, and I did not treat general demographic statistics as representative of this specific community. I also checked each quotation and reference against its original source.
5. How did you maintain your authentic voice in the work?
I treated Claude’s suggestions as drafts to question rather than answers to accept. I checked the research question, the choice of participants and the interpretations against the assignment requirements and the supporting evidence, and then revised the material so that it reflects my own understanding of community participation, researcher positionality, and the relationships between citizen scientists and technology.
